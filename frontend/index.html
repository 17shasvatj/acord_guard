STEP 1 — BACKGROUND The situation, and the documents the AI is given to work from.

The client file

A small insurance agency (Bayline Insurance Services) and its records for one commercial customer, Paws and Provisions LLC. Everything the AI is allowed to know lives in these documents — you can open each one.

The customer's insurance policy (the “declarations page”)

The official summary of a business's coverage: which protections they carry, the dollar limits (the most the insurer will pay), and from when until when the coverage runs. This policy carries general-liability coverage — but no add-ons (“endorsements”) that would extend that coverage to anyone else, and no cyber coverage.

View the policy ↗

A certificate request

A general contractor, Acme, won't let Paws and Provisions work on its site without proof of insurance. Acme asks for a certificate — and wants to be named as “additional insured” with a “waiver of subrogation,” two legal protections that only exist if the policy actually carries the matching endorsements. This one doesn't. Hold onto that — it's the exact thing the stress test at the end puts to the test.

Read the request ↗

A certificate of insurance is one page an insured business hands to a client or landlord as proof of coverage. Whoever receives it relies on the dollar amounts, the dates, and the checked boxes to be true — they let the business onto the job on the strength of that paper. If a value is wrong, the party relying on it is exposed, and the agency that issued it is liable.

STEP 2 — THE PROBLEM What Gail's certificate skill does with that file today, and where it goes wrong.

What GailGPT produced from these documents

We asked GailGPT — Gail's certificate assistant — to generate a certificate of insurance from the documents in Step 1, and we ran it many times across two AI models: the one Gail uses by default (Gemini 3.7 Flash) and its most capable option (Opus 5). The point was to see whether simply using a smarter model fixes the problem. It doesn't. Two things went wrong; the runs that produced each are labeled on the cards below. Highlighted values appear nowhere in the policy — the AI supplied them.

PROBLEM 1 · CERTIFIED EXPIRED COVERAGE · DEFAULT MODEL (GEMINI 3.7 FLASH)

It issued a certificate on coverage that had already expired

Given the policy whose term ran 01/22/2025 – 01/22/2026, with a certificate date of 08/25/2026 — coverage lapsed seven months earlier — the default model (Gemini 3.7 Flash) produced a complete, downloadable ACORD 25 certifying that coverage, and said nothing about the expiration. Whether coverage is in force on the certificate date is the single most important thing the document exists to prove. A contractor relying on this certificate would believe Paws and Provisions was covered. It wasn't.

PROBLEM 2 · INVENTED A REGULATORY ID · BOTH MODELS, EVERY RUN

It invented the insurer's NAIC number

the policy names only “The Hartford” — it has no NAIC number
Insurer: Hartford Fire Insurance Company   NAIC #: 19682

A NAIC number is a government regulator's unique ID for an insurance company. The policy on file doesn't contain one — the AI supplied it from memory and printed it on the certificate as if it were fact. It happens to be a real number, which is worse, not better: a confident, plausible, unverifiable value in the insurer field of a legal document. Both models did this, on every run.

THE PATTERN · WHY A BIGGER MODEL ISN'T THE FIX

A better model doesn't fix this

You might expect the expensive model to be the safe one. It isn't, consistently. On the expired policy, Opus 5 did catch the problem and refuse — but the default model (Gemini 3.7 Flash), the one that runs at volume, issued the certificate silently. And the invented NAIC number appeared on both models, every single run — the most capable one included. So upgrading the model bought you one catch, on one check, and nothing else. The behavior you can't predict in advance is the behavior you can't rely on. What a legal document needs isn't a smarter writer — it's a check that runs the same way every time, regardless of which model wrote the values or how expensive it was. That check is Step 3.

STEP 3 — THE FIX The verification layer, running live. Pick a policy and watch every value get checked.

Now run the checking layer on the same file — live

Every value that reaches the certificate has to trace back to a real sentence in the source policy, and deterministic code makes the final call. The layer approves a certificate when the policy backs everything on it, and stops one when it doesn't — here, an expired policy, and fabricated values forced straight into the checker.

Your policy replaces the sample; the certificate request stays fixed. The model reads your policy for the coverage dates, limits, carrier, and — crucially — whether the endorsements exist to support the requested boxes. If the PDF is a scan with no text layer, you'll see every field come back “not found” — that's the layer refusing to guess on something it can't read, not a bug.

The stress test — answering Acme's request the hard way

Back in Step 1, Acme asked to be named additional insured with a waiver of subrogation — and this policy carries neither endorsement. So what stops a certificate from claiming them anyway? This run finds out. It bypasses the AI entirely and force-feeds the checker the values a careless system might produce: those two endorsement boxes checked “yes,” plus an invented NAIC number and a made-up producer code. The question is simply “can a value the policy doesn't support get onto the certificate?” Watch each one get blocked below — the endorsement boxes on the specific grounds that the policy carries no such endorsement, so it cannot be certified. That is the protection Acme's request needed, enforced by code instead of left to the model's judgment.
